Topic notes

1. Basic Concepts of Musculoskeletal System

★★★☆☆R 2 · B 1 · C 3
  • Four tissue systems of the MSK system: skeletal (bones), articular (joints, cartilage), muscular, and neuromuscular (nerves supplying muscle).
  • Bone functions — mnemonic "SPMCH": Support, Protection, Movement (levers), Calcium/mineral storage, Haematopoiesis.
  • 206 bones: axial skeleton (80 — skull, vertebral column, rib cage) + appendicular (126 — limbs and girdles).
  • Bone classification (shape): long, short, flat, irregular, sesamoid; microstructure: cortical (compact, ~80% of bone mass) vs cancellous (trabecular, metabolically active).
  • Wolff's law: bone is laid down where needed and resorbed where not needed — bone remodels along lines of stress. Converse: disuse/immobilisation causes osteoporosis.
  • Joint classification — structural: fibrous, cartilaginous, synovial; functional: synarthrosis (immovable), amphiarthrosis (slightly movable), diarthrosis (freely movable).
  • Synovial joint components: articular (hyaline) cartilage, capsule, synovial membrane, and accessory structures (labrum, menisci, discs, fat pads, bursae).
  • Cartilage types: hyaline (articular), fibro (menisci, TMJ disc), elastic (ear, epiglottis). Articular cartilage is avascular, aneural, aneural nutrition by synovial diffusion.
  • Muscle fibre types: Type I (slow oxidative, endurance, red), Type IIa (fast oxidative-glycolytic), Type IIx (fast glycolytic, fatigable).
  • Neuromuscular basis: motor unit (anterior horn cell + axon + fibres), muscle spindle (length/velocity), Golgi tendon organ (tension), alpha-gamma co-activation, stretch reflex.
Examiner’s view

This unit is the vocabulary of the whole course. Examiners commonly ask for the functional classification of joints, types of cartilage and their clinical relevance (e.g., why articular cartilage heals poorly), Wolff's law applied to fracture healing and immobilisation, and muscle fibre types with training implications. Expect a short-note rather than a long essay from this unit.

2. Basic Concepts of Anthropometry

★★☆☆☆R 1 · B 1 · C 2
  • Definition: the science of systematic measurement of the human body — size, shape, composition and proportions.
  • Tools: stadiometer (height), anthropometer, spreading and sliding calipers, skinfold calipers, measuring tape, weighing scale.
  • Body size measures: height, weight, BMI (kg/m²), segmental lengths, girths; body shape: somatotypes — endomorph, mesomorph, ectomorph.
  • Body composition methods — mnemonic "D-H-BS" (Most to least accurate): DEXA (or multicomponent models), Hydrodensitometry (underwater weighing), Bioelectrical impedance, Skinfolds (BIA and skinfolds are roughly comparable).
  • MSK applications: limb length measurement (direct — tape between bony landmarks; indirect — blocks under the short limb to level the pelvis), girth for muscle atrophy, waist-hip ratio.
  • Human variation: intra-individual (bilateral), inter-individual, population-level; relevance to ergonomic fit and prosthetic/orthotic sizing.
Examiner’s view

A low-yield unit that usually appears as a short note. Learn the definition, the measurement tools, and the body composition techniques in order of accuracy. Its clinical hook in MSK practice is limb-length discrepancy assessment and its effect on pelvic alignment and gait.

3. Basic Concepts of Biomechanics

★★★★★R 4 · B 1 · C 5
  • Kinematics = description of motion (displacement, velocity, acceleration) without reference to forces; Kinetics = study of the forces that cause motion.
  • Force is a vector: magnitude, direction, point of application, line of action.
  • Newton's laws: (1) inertia, (2) F = ma, (3) action-reaction (basis of the ground reaction force).
  • Moment of force (torque) = Force x perpendicular distance from the axis (unit Nm). A force couple = two equal, opposite, non-collinear parallel forces producing pure rotation.
  • Equilibrium: static and dynamic; first condition (sum of forces = 0), second condition (sum of moments = 0). Centre of gravity and base of support determine stability.
  • Lever classes — mnemonic "1-2-3 = F-R-E" (the middle element in each class): 1st class Fulcrum in the middle (atlanto-occipital joint, seesaw); 2nd class Resistance in the middle (ankle plantarflexors during heel raise — mechanical advantage > 1); 3rd class Effort in the middle (most human joints, e.g., elbow flexion — mechanical advantage < 1, built for speed and range).
  • Degrees of freedom: number of independent planes of motion (e.g., knee — 2 DoF; shoulder — 3 DoF).
  • Energetics: work (J), power (W), efficiency (muscular efficiency ~20-25%); most economical walking speed is around 80 m/min (about 4.8 km/h); metabolic energy consumption measured via oxygen uptake.
Examiner’s view

This is the highest-priority foundation topic — its concepts reappear in joint mechanics, gait, posture and rehabilitation methods, which is why it earns five stars on repetition plus one blueprint question. Classic exam asks: define and classify levers with one example of each; differentiate kinematics from kinetics with gait examples; define moment with a clinical example (e.g., patellofemoral joint reaction force rises as the knee flexes because the moment arm of body weight increases).

4. Biomechanics of Movement across the Life Span

★★☆☆☆R 2 · B 0 · C 2

study together with Gait

  • MSK growth and development: endochondral ossification, epiphyseal growth plates (vulnerable in paediatric trauma — Salter-Harris classification), bone modelling/remodelling.
  • Maturation of mobility and gait in children: independent walking at ~12 months; early gait = wide base, high cadence, short stride, arms held high without reciprocal swing, flat-footed strike, absent heel-toe pattern; most kinematics are adult-like by 3-4 years, with full maturation (velocity, stride length, energy cost) by about 7-8 years.
  • Gait changes in older adults: slower speed, shorter step length, reduced cadence, reduced push-off (ankle power), increased double-limb support time, stooped posture; a wider base of support is classically described (textbook convention — empirical evidence is mixed).
  • Biomechanics of ageing: sarcopenia, decreased bone mineral density, joint stiffness, slower reaction time, reduced balance reserve.
Examiner’s view

The blueprint does not allot this topic a dedicated question (it is examined through the Gait topic in the same paper), so read it jointly with Gait and Function. The exam angle is always a comparison: gait of a toddler versus a healthy elderly subject, or growth-plate injury and its consequences. Do not spend disproportionate time here.

5. Physiological Basis of Human Movement

★★★☆☆R 2 · B 1 · C 3
  • Exercise metabolism — three energy systems, mnemonic "Seconds, Minutes, Hours": phosphagen/ATP-PC system (0-10 seconds, anaerobic), anaerobic glycolysis (up to ~2 minutes, lactate), oxidative/aerobic system (sustained activity).
  • Oxygen supply during exercise: Fick principle — VO2 = cardiac output x arteriovenous O2 difference; VO2max as the index of aerobic capacity.
  • Energy cost of activity: METs; 1 MET = 3.5 ml O2/kg/min. Examples: sitting 1-2, slow walking 2-3, brisk walking 3-4, climbing stairs 4-5+... (learn 5-6 common values).
  • Measurement of exercise capacity: graded exercise tests (GXT), submaximal tests (e.g., 6-minute walk test, shuttle walk), RPE — Borg 6-20 and 0-10 scales.
  • Adaptations: cardiovascular (increased stroke volume, decreased resting HR), muscular (hypertrophy, fibre transition, mitochondrial density), neural (early strength gains are neural).
  • Application in MSK disease: disuse atrophy, deconditioning, chronic pain-related inactivity; exercise as medicine.
Examiner’s view

Answer format for a long question: define each energy system with its substrate, duration and example sport/activity; then Fick's principle; then METs with clinical application to activity prescription in MSK rehabilitation. This unit links directly to exercise testing in Paper III and endurance interventions in Paper IV.

6. Mechanics and Pathomechanics of Joints

★★★★★R 3 · B 2 · C 5
  • Osteokinematics = angular movements of bones (flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, rotation); Arthrokinematics = joint surface motions — roll, glide (slide), spin.
  • Concave-convex rule — mnemonic "Convex-Contrary, Concave-Concordant": a convex surface moving on a fixed concave surface glides OPPOSITE to the direction of roll (e.g., humeral head in shoulder abduction); a concave surface moving on a fixed convex surface glides in the SAME direction as the roll (e.g., tibia on femur in open-chain knee extension).
  • Close-packed position (maximal congruency, taut capsule and ligaments) vs loose/open-packed (resting) position — learn for major joints: knee — full extension is close-packed; shoulder — full abduction with external rotation is close-packed (a commonly quoted loose-packed position is 55° abduction with 30° horizontal adduction).
  • Upper limb: shoulder complex — scapulohumeral rhythm 2:1 (glenohumeral to scapular motion averaged over the full arc of elevation 0-180°; the first ~30° is a variable "setting phase" dominated by glenohumeral motion), rotator cuff force couple (deltoid vs supraspinatus/infraspinatus/teres minor/subscapularis), and impingement pathomechanics; elbow carrying angle (valgus ~11° men, ~13° women); forearm pronation-supination (axis through radial head to ulnar styloid); wrist and hand joint architecture.
  • Lower limb: femoral neck anteversion (adult average ~10-12°, range 8-15°), Q-angle and patellofemoral stress; knee screw-home mechanism (terminal extension locks the knee with tibial external rotation — "locked and screwed"); arches of the foot (medial longitudinal, transverse) and windlass mechanism.
  • Spine: coupled movements (side-flexion with rotation — the direction of the coupled rotation differs by region and spine position — learn the classic coupled patterns), segmental motion of the lumbar spine, neutral spine concept.
  • TMJ: opening combines pure rotation (to ~20-25 mm) followed by translation (to ~35-55 mm) with the mandibular midline staying central; deviation of the midline on opening is a sign of dysfunction (typically disc displacement with reduction).
  • Skeletal tissue mechanics: stress-strain curve (toe, elastic/linear, plastic, failure regions), viscoelastic behaviour (creep, stress relaxation, hysteresis), anisotropy.
  • Soft tissue mechanics: ligament/tendon behaviour, immobilisation weakens connective tissue; creep under sustained load.
Examiner’s view

Carrying two blueprint questions and one of the largest topic loads in the paper, this is guaranteed marks territory. Examiners love the concave-convex rule with named examples, close-packed positions, scapulohumeral rhythm, and the screw-home mechanism. Practice drawing the stress-strain curve and labelling its regions — a frequent diagram question.

7. Posture

★★★☆☆R 2 · B 1 · C 3
  • Definition: the relative alignment of body segments at a given time; ideal static posture = minimal muscle work and minimal joint stress with the line of gravity passing through key landmarks.
  • Plumb line alignment (lateral): external auditory meatus — acromion — greater trochanter — just anterior to knee joint axis — just anterior to lateral malleolus.
  • Postural control — mnemonic "VVS": Vision, Vestibular, Somatosensory (proprioceptive) inputs integrated by the CNS; sensorimotor strategies: ankle strategy, hip strategy, stepping strategy; anticipatory (feed-forward) vs reactive (feedback) postural adjustments.
  • Analysis: static observation (anterior, posterior, lateral), plumb line, postural grids, goniometry for fixed deviations, functional tests.
  • Postural deformities: forward head posture, kyphosis, lordosis, scoliosis (screen with the Adams forward bend test; structural vs functional), pelvic tilt abnormalities, genu valgum/varum, pes planus/cavus.
  • Distinguish structural (fixed, bony) from functional (flexible, postural/habitual) deformities — determines treatment.
  • McKenzie postural syndrome: sustained end-range loading causes pain in the absence of pathology; corrected by posture and regular movement.
Examiner’s view

Expect either a short note on postural control mechanisms or an essay on postural assessment and common deformities. The plumb-line landmarks, the three sensory inputs, and the structural-versus-functional distinction are the marks. Relate posture to sitting ergonomics (Paper IV) and to gait (same paper).

8. Gait and Function

★★★★☆R 3 · B 1 · C 4
  • Gait cycle (Rancho Los Amigos terminology): stance 60%, swing 40%. Eight phases — stance: initial contact, loading response, mid-stance, terminal stance, pre-swing; swing: initial, mid-, terminal swing.
  • Mnemonic for the eight phases in order: "In Loading, My Terms Precede Initials Mid-Travel" (Initial contact, Loading response, Mid-stance, Terminal stance, Pre-swing, Initial swing, Mid-swing, Terminal swing).
  • Six determinants of gait (Saunders, Inman, Eberhart) reduce the vertical/lateral excursion of the centre of mass: (1) pelvic rotation, (2) pelvic tilt (list), (3) knee flexion in stance, (4) foot and ankle mechanisms, (5) knee mechanisms... — learn as: pelvic rotation, pelvic tilt, knee flexion in stance, foot mechanism, ankle mechanism, pelvic lateral displacement.
  • Muscle function by phase: tibialis anterior (eccentric in loading response), quadriceps (loading response, pre-swing), gluteus medius/minimus (mid-stance — pelvic control), gluteus maximus (initial contact/loading), hamstrings (decelerate swing at terminal swing), gastrocnemius-soleus (terminal stance — A2 push-off).
  • Pathological gaits (learn at least six with cause): Trendelenburg (weak hip abductors — contralateral pelvic drop); antalgic (short stance on painful limb); high-stepping/steppage (foot drop — tibialis anterior weakness); hemiplegic (circumduction, equinus); parkinsonian (festinating, stooped, reduced arm swing); ataxic (broad-based, irregular); gluteus maximus gait (thrusting trunk backwards).
  • Energy expenditure: walking is most economical near 80 m/min; lower-limb amputation raises the oxygen cost per metre by roughly 40-100% depending on level and cause (classic Waters et al. data: traumatic transfemoral ~+41%, vascular transfemoral ~+102%, traumatic transtibial ~+12%).
  • Kinetic and kinematic analysis of functional tasks: sit-to-stand (momentum strategy, arm use), reaching, lifting, stair ascent/descent.
Examiner’s view

This is one of the most reliable exam topics across the whole course — definitions of the gait cycle and its phases, the determinants, and named pathological gaits with their lesions. The diagram of the gait cycle with muscle activity must be practised until it can be drawn from memory. Combine your reading with the life-span biomechanics topic.

9. Pain

★★★★★R 3 · B 2 · C 5
  • IASP definition (2020): "An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage."
  • Four neurophysiological processes — mnemonic "TTMP": Transduction (nociceptor activation), Transmission (A-delta and C fibres — spinothalamic tract), Modulation (descending inhibition — gate control, endogenous opioids), Perception (cortical limbic experience).
  • Nociceptor fibres: A-delta (fast, sharp, well localised — neospinothalamic) and C (slow, dull, burning — paleospinothalamic).
  • Theories of pain: Specificity theory (von Frey), Pattern theory (Goldscheider/Wedel... historically Wedell/Melzack), Gate Control theory (Melzack and Wall, 1965) — the theory that revolutionised pain science: substantia gelatinosa gate; large-diameter fibres close the gate, small-fibre activity opens it.
  • Acute vs chronic pain: timeline (>3 months), chronic pain is a disease of the nervous system — peripheral and central sensitisation (allodynia, hyperalgesia); chronic pain has lost its warning function.
  • Assessment in different populations: VAS (0-10 cm), Numerical Rating Scale, McGill Pain Questionnaire (sensory, affective, evaluative dimensions), body pain diagram, Faces scale (children), observational tools for non-communicative patients (e.g., PAINAD for dementia).
  • Specific states and syndromes: neuropathic pain (DN4 screening), radicular pain, complex regional pain syndrome, myofascial pain syndrome (trigger points) vs fibromyalgia, phantom limb pain.
  • Evidence-based advances: biopsychosocial model, Pain Neuroscience Education, fear-avoidance model (Vlaeyen), central sensitisation inventory.
Examiner’s view

Pain recurs across all three MSK papers — physiology here, red-flag/referred pain screening in Paper III, and multimodal management in Paper IV — which is why it carries a maximum combined rating. Standard long-answer structure: definition, pain pathways (with a labelled diagram of the gate control theory), theories, acute-versus-chronic differences in a table, assessment tools, and a conclusion on the biopsychosocial model. The gate control theory with its clinical applications (rubbing an injury, TENS) is the single most repeated question.

10. Electro Physics

★★★★★R 3 · B 2 · C 5
  • Basic concepts: types of current (direct, alternating, pulsed), key parameters — frequency (Hz), intensity/amplitude (mA), pulse duration/phase (microseconds or ms), duty cycle (on:off time), waveform.
  • Alterations in skeletal muscle performance: disuse atrophy; electrical stimulation of muscle (NMES) as an adjunct to voluntary exercise — strength, endurance, and torque parameters (Russian currents: 2500 Hz burst-modulated... commonly 50 bps for strengthening).
  • Soft tissue repair phases: inflammation, proliferation, remodelling — each with its electro/physical modality of choice.
  • Biophysical effects of heat — modes of transfer, mnemonic "CCCR": Conduction (hot pack, wax), Convection (whirlpool, fluidotherapy), Conversion (ultrasound, diathermy — energy converted to heat in tissues), Radiation (infrared lamp).
  • Physiological effects of heat: vasodilation, increased metabolism, altered nerve conduction velocity (pain relief), increased collagen extensibility (with stretch); of cold: vasoconstriction, decreased metabolism (limits secondary injury in acute inflammation... reduces swelling), decreased nerve conduction, pain relief.
  • Clinical decision-making in modality selection: goal (pain, healing, strength?), tissue depth, stage of healing, contraindications/precautions, patient preference, evidence.
  • Alternative modalities for pain and tissue healing: low-level laser therapy (photobiomodulation), extracorporeal shockwave therapy, magnetic therapy.
Examiner’s view

This unit secures its top rating by spanning the entire syllabus — the same physics returns in electrodiagnosis (Paper III) and electro modalities in rehabilitation (Paper IV). Exam favourites: parameter definitions, physiological effects of heat and cold, and the decision-making rationale for choosing a modality. Learn contraindications cold (pacemakers, malignancy over the area, impaired sensation, pregnancy over the abdomen/pelvis, acute recent bleeding).

Glossary of difficult terms (35)

Dotted-underlined words also show the definition on hover or tap.

  • allodynia — Pain from a stimulus that is not normally painful.
  • arthrokinematics — The small gliding, rolling and spinning movements between joint surfaces.
  • atrophy — Wasting or shrinking of muscle or tissue.
  • biomechanics — The study of the mechanical laws relating to the movement of living organisms.
  • biopsychosocial — An approach that considers biological, psychological and social factors together.
  • capsule — The fibrous sleeve that surrounds and stabilises a joint.
  • central sensitisation — Increased excitability of the central nervous system that makes normal inputs feel painful.
  • collagen — The main structural protein in tendon, ligament, skin and bone.
  • creep — Slow progressive lengthening of tissue under a constant sustained load over time.
  • eccentric — Muscle work while it lengthens under load (the lowering phase).
  • fear-avoidance — Avoiding activity because of fear of pain, which over time worsens disability.
  • gait — The manner of walking.
  • gate control theory — The idea that non-painful input (rubbing, TENS) can 'close a gate' in the spinal cord and reduce pain.
  • golgi tendon organ — A sensor in tendon that detects tension and helps regulate muscle force.
  • goniometry — Measuring joint angles with a goniometer.
  • hyperalgesia — An increased sensitivity to pain.
  • hypertrophy — An increase in muscle or tissue size.
  • inflammation — The body's protective response to injury - heat, redness, swelling and pain.
  • laser therapy — Red/near-infrared light therapy that stimulates cell repair (photobiomodulation).
  • ligament — Strong fibrous tissue that connects bone to bone and stabilises a joint.
  • line of gravity — The imaginary vertical line through the body's centre of gravity, used to analyse posture.
  • moment arm — The perpendicular distance from a force to the joint axis; a longer moment arm produces more torque.
  • muscle spindle — A stretch sensor inside muscle that helps control posture and movement.
  • myofascial — Relating to muscle and its surrounding fascia.
  • nociceptor — A sensory nerve ending that detects painful or tissue-damaging stimuli.
  • osteokinematics — The visible movements of bones at a joint, such as flexion and extension.
  • posture — The position of the body and its parts relative to gravity.
  • proprioceptive — Relating to the body's sense of position and movement.
  • specificity — A test's ability to correctly identify people who do not have the condition.
  • stress relaxation — The fall in tissue tension when it is held at a fixed length over time.
  • stress-strain curve — A graph showing how much a tissue deforms (strain) as load (stress) is applied.
  • synovial — Relating to the fluid-filled lining of a joint.
  • tendon — Strong fibrous tissue that attaches muscle to bone.
  • TENS — Transcutaneous Electrical Nerve Stimulation - a mild current passed through the skin to relieve pain.
  • torque — A turning force around an axis - the product of force and its moment arm.
